Designing the Structure and Style of an Interactive Experience

We began designing our UI/UX experience around the overwhelmed 1st year teacher in need of a quick 2 minute wellness break during the school day. Based on research stored on a Gemini Notebook, and followup AI chats to refine the design vision, an AGENTS.md document was compiled to frame AI activity when designing the ‘3R’ wellness experience. 

I started contributing to the design by drawing on my experience of teaching kindergarten. If the kids weren't ready for an activity, the distractions would take over, the room would get very noisy and the kids who were really keen would get frustrated. In those situations I would need a moment to check in with my own feelings, to refocus and to be present for the class. 
I realised this was very much the same as the strategies I'd taught kids when they were feeling overwhelmed (take a breath, name the feeling, think of something positive, talk to someone you trust). That took us straight to our core question: "How can this app help me take a breath, identify what I'm feeling and then get back to my students with a clear head?" 
The Reflect, Release, Recharge framework was really useful in structuring these thoughts. For the Recharge section I was looking for some supportive humour and some teacher movie moments. The aim was to reassure teachers and to remind them that one difficult lesson doesn't in any way take away from the great relationships you can build with your students.

Gemini worked best at aligning educational theory, specifically best practices for micro wellness activities for the 1st year teacher, with how the HTML and CSS code should be developed. For example: 
putting the entire <body> within a <div>, with only a <header>, <body> and <footer> within the <main> canvass, limited to a 1080 x 1350 px Instagram format. The rest of the CSS layout could then be completely adaptive/responsive using rems and clamps(). 
All of this code design was generated in Gemini, which did the best job of linking academic best practices to the design of the UI/UX, e.g., don’t give the overloaded teacher more than one choice to think about when engaging in the microwellness phone app. 
When moving to collaborative production activities, Codex and CoPilot were the AI platforms used. The /ver1 files were used initially in the production stage, but quickly became evident that more strict criteria had to be written into AGENTS.md. In the /chat documents, a finetuning of AGENTS, INDEX, STYLE and SCRIPT was generated. 

I worked with Codex to take my classroom experience and design ideas and turn them into an interactive prototype. I asked for a short emotional check-in, a guided breathing exercise, some practical encouragement for the classroom and a few movie clips. Codex gave me the code and interface options which enabled me to see how those elements might come together. 
As I asked for more features the prototype grew into a much larger website with several content areas. When we looked at it in the context of Robert's framework we saw it didn't always adhere to the fixed layout and simple experience that we'd wanted. That was the moment we realised that even useful features can add up and make things more onerous for the teacher. 
Next I asked Codex to go through AGENTS.md (the 3Rs document), the wireframes and update the prototype to use a fixed 4:5 app format. It was also to check the buttons, timing, layout and movie links. The new version of the prototype presented the experience as a series of screens rather than a dashboard. 
My job was to provide classroom context, check that the language and activities were appropriate and supportive and flag up where the website/app needed changing (e.g. add in some fun activities or videos). Codex dealt with the coding and the updates. The whole process has reinforced the need for regular human review of AI outputs even when the output looks good on the surface. 
 
I built the prototype using HTML, CSS and JavaScript to link the check-in, the calming activity and the Recharge content. The teacher's choice would determine the supportive messages and there was a timer to guide through the steps. I also included optional links to scenes from School of Rock and Freedom Writers to illustrate the level of student engagement and sense of belonging. 
The prototype passed 14 automated checks and I used Codex to help us go through the key interactions on both desktop and mobile. The checks were all around how it worked and how it looked, they didn't really tell us whether it actually reduced teacher stress. Testing on actual phones and getting feedback from teachers would be the next logical step. 
We tried two different approaches in our versions. My prototype had the options such as pause and choose grounding, Robert's version had far fewer options and just carried on. We went with Robert's version for the final project. I've left my prototype, the documentation and the test notes in my repo as a record of our development process.
